This research file begins with constitutive law, parliamentary records and formal administrative instruments. Museum-authored histories are not used here to establish substantive historical propositions where upstream evidence can be recovered.

Evidence rule: absence is visible. Where SIDONIE has not yet recovered the relevant founding deed, Treasury minute, parliamentary paper or archival record, the page says so rather than filling the gap from later institutional commentary.
1953–54
PRIMARY — PARLIAMENTARY RECORD

Reform debated in Parliament

Documented fact

The National Art Collections Bill debates reconstruct the institutional position of the National Gallery and Tate immediately before post-war statutory reform.

Why it matters

This provides contemporaneous evidence for why governance reform was being considered, rather than a retrospective institutional narrative.

What remained / evidentiary limit

The later 1992 Act supersedes the earlier statutory machinery but not the historical evidence of the reform debate.

1992
PRIMARY — STATUTE

Modern statutory settlement

Documented fact

The Museums and Galleries Act 1992 establishes the modern Tate Board/functions and its legal relationship with the National Gallery.

Why it matters

The reciprocal trustee relationship is a statutory/governance fact, not merely an institutional custom.

What remained / evidentiary limit

Tate and National Gallery remain separately governed bodies.

Current sponsor framework

Documented fact

The current DCMS framework spells out appointments, accountability and spending controls.

Why it matters

It makes contemporary administrative leverage visible while remaining subordinate to statute.

What remained / evidentiary limit

The 1992 Act remains the constitutive baseline.
