Culture at Work · Institution over time

National Gallery:
institutional genealogy.

From a 38-picture Treasury purchase to a statutory national collection.

1824
Foundation / funding

National Gallery established

Documented change

Government purchases Angerstein's 38 paintings for £57,000 and establishes a national collection.

Why it matters

A private collection becomes publicly owned; public money, access, security and conservation are linked from the start.

What remained

The core idea of a national collection for public benefit persists.

Evidence: NG1;NG2
1828
Governance

Trustee Board begins regular operation

Documented change

A Treasury-appointed committee develops into a functioning Board of Trustees.

Why it matters

Creates an enduring layer between government and institutional administration.

What remained

The state remains financially and constitutionally upstream.

Evidence: NG3
1853–55
Governance / management

Directorate and professionalisation

Documented change

Parliamentary scrutiny retains trustees and establishes the Director role; specialist functions later expand.

Why it matters

Authority becomes differentiated among trustees, Director and professional staff.

What remained

Trusteeship survives rather than direct departmental management.

Evidence: NG3;NG4
1954–55
Governance

Formal Tate cross-representation

Documented change

The Gallery and Tate acquire reciprocal board representation.

Why it matters

Creates an unusual institutional bridge that survives today.

What remained

Each institution remains separately governed.

Evidence: NG3
1992
Law / governance

Museums and Galleries Act

Documented change

The Act incorporates the modern Board and sets its functions.

Why it matters

Modernises the statutory architecture and preserves Tate cross-appointment.

What remained

The Gallery remains an NDPB/exempt charity rather than a government department.

Evidence: NG5;NG6
2025–26
Governance / finance

Current framework architecture

Documented change

The framework specifies current accountability, appointment and spending arrangements with DCMS.

Why it matters

Shows how trustee autonomy coexists with policy frameworks, PM appointments and delegated spending controls.

What remained

The framework does not replace the 1992 Act.

Evidence: NG6
